
2.	Methodology

2.1 Research design and rationale

This study addressed the extent to which superior renewable resource endowment could compensate for higher financing costs in green hydrogen production, and the conditions under which this compensation broke down. Rather than ranking countries or sites, the design isolated two effects that the literature typically treats separately, physical resource quality and cost of capital, and recombined them in a controlled counterfactual framework to identify an inversion point: a financing-cost threshold at which the ranking between a resource-rich, capital-constrained region (Northeast Brazil) and a resource-modest, capital-abundant region (North Germany) reversed.

The analysis proceeded in four stages: spatial suitability assessment to establish where hydrogen hubs could plausibly be sited in each region; technical potential estimation for the resulting hubs; a levelised-cost-of-hydrogen (LCOH) model applied under actual, counterfactual, and inverted financing conditions; and a sensitivity and inversion-point analysis linking the financial gap to policy-relevant thresholds.

Northeast Brazil and North Germany (Schleswig-Holstein and Niedersachsen) were selected as a paired case because they represented a near-maximal contrast in resource endowment, with Global Horizontal Irradiance of approximately 1,800 to 2,200 kWh/m2/yr in Northeast Brazil versus 950 to 1,100 kWh/m2/yr in North Germany, combined with a near-inverse contrast in financing conditions, in which the weighted average cost of capital was markedly higher in Brazil than in Germany for equivalent renewable asset classes. This made the pair analytically efficient for a decomposition design, since the two effects pointed in opposite directions, the necessary condition for an inversion point to exist at all.

2.2 Data sources and acquisition strategy

The pipeline was designed to acquire the large majority of input data automatically through public application programming interfaces or bulk download endpoints, minimizing recurring manual intervention. Where full automation was not feasible because of the access policy of the providing institution, rather than an engineering limitation, a one-time manual credential setup was required; this was distinct from recurring manual downloads, which the pipeline did not require for any source.

2.3 Spatial suitability modelling (GIS-MCDA)

Site suitability was assessed using the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) as the primary multi-criteria method, applied independently to each region and each renewable technology on the common 1 km grid. TOPSIS is distance-based and admitted literature-derived criterion weights directly, without requiring the pairwise expert elicitation used in the Analytic Hierarchy Process, a step associated in the siting literature with low inter-study agreement and susceptibility to subjective, non-representative weighting. The Visekriterijumska Optimizacija I Kompromisno Resenje method (VIKOR) was applied as an independent robustness cross-check against the TOPSIS ranking. This cross-check confirms ranking stability for onshore wind, with a Spearman rank correlation between the two methods of 0.91 in Northeast Brazil and 0.80 in North Germany, both comfortably indicating agreement. For solar, the two methods show materially weaker concordance, 0.38 in Northeast Brazil and 0.15 in North Germany, reflecting a genuine, diagnosed divergence between TOPSIS's and VIKOR's aggregation behaviour rather than an implementation defect: TOPSIS normalizes each criterion by its own magnitude across all candidate cells, a step that is directly sensitive to that criterion's spatial coefficient of variation, whereas VIKOR normalizes each criterion by its own observed minimum and maximum, a step that always rescales a criterion to its full relative range regardless of how spatially uniform its underlying values are. Solar irradiance is spatially far more uniform across each study region than wind power density is, so TOPSIS's normalization leaves solar's resource-quality criterion contributing only a small share of the ranking's actual discriminating variance despite carrying the largest nominal weight, while VIKOR continues to weight it near its full nominal share; the two methods are consequently optimizing meaningfully different effective objectives for solar, though not for wind, where the resource criterion retains enough spatial variance to remain influential under both normalization schemes. Given this, the solar site ranking reported here is derived from TOPSIS alone, with the VIKOR comparison retained as a wind-specific robustness confirmation and as a documented, diagnosed divergence for solar rather than a validated agreement.

A related diagnostic, distinct from the TOPSIS-VIKOR concordance check above, is the direct correlation between each technology's own resource-quality criterion and the final TOPSIS suitability score it produces. Measured directly against the on-disk criterion and suitability rasters, this correlation is low for solar (Pearson r = 0.12, Northeast Brazil; r = -0.07, North Germany) and moderate to strong for onshore wind (r = 0.75, Northeast Brazil; r = 0.58, North Germany). This is the same underlying mechanism identified above, now expressed as its direct consequence: solar irradiance's coefficient of variation across each study region's valid cells is approximately 6 percent in Northeast Brazil and 1.5 percent in North Germany, an order of magnitude lower than onshore wind's power-density coefficient of variation over the same cells (approximately 73 percent and 24 percent, respectively). Vector normalization, TOPSIS's standard procedure and the one retained throughout this study, divides each criterion by its own Euclidean norm across all candidate cells; simulation and empirical comparisons of normalization procedures for TOPSIS and related distance-based multi-criteria methods have found vector normalization to produce more consistent rankings and lower sensitivity to weight perturbation than minimum-maximum normalization (Chakraborty and Yeh, 2009; Jahan and Edwards, 2015), a preference confirmed specifically for TOPSIS by a dedicated comparative line of work (Vafaei, Ribeiro and Camarinha-Matos, 2016, 2018, 2021). A direct, mechanical consequence of vector normalization is that a criterion with very low spatial coefficient of variation contributes little to the normalized decision matrix's discriminating variance regardless of its nominal weight, while a criterion with high spatial coefficient of variation contributes close to its full nominal share (same sources); this is documented, expected behaviour of the method under low-dispersion inputs, not an artefact of this study's implementation.

As an explicit robustness check, not a candidate replacement for the primary method, TOPSIS was re-run for each region and technology with an adaptive rule substituting minimum-maximum normalization for any criterion whose coefficient of variation falls below 0.10 (solar's resource criterion in both regions only). This raised the resource-suitability correlation for solar to effectively 1.0 in both regions, but did so by rendering the final suitability score numerically indistinguishable from the minimum-maximum-normalized resource criterion alone (maximum absolute per-cell deviation of 0.005 for Northeast Brazil solar), annihilating the intended influence of slope, grid distance, and water proximity, three criteria carrying a combined 65 percent of nominal weight. This is the mirror-image outcome of the low-correlation pattern it was meant to probe, not a methodological improvement over vector normalization, and is retained in the underlying codebase only as a documented robustness artefact, not as the ranking reported here. Minimum-maximum normalization is nonetheless the more common default in applied GIS-MCDA renewable-siting studies specifically (Villacreses et al., 2017; Sanchez-Lozano, Garcia-Cascales and Lamata, 2016; Latinopoulos and Kechagia, 2015; Doorga et al., 2019; Aydin, Kentel and Duzgun, 2013; Al-Shammari et al., 2021), while vector normalization is more often preferred in the literature evaluating normalization procedures for TOPSIS in the abstract (references above); no single recommendation spans both bodies of work, and reviews of decision-support methods applied more broadly to renewable energy investment have found that different methods tend to converge on similar top-ranked alternatives even where intermediate scores diverge (Strantzali and Aravossis, 2016). This study follows the normalization-procedure-specific literature and retains vector normalization as primary, reporting the low solar resource-suitability correlation as a documented, diagnosed property of the ranking under low resource-criterion variance, in the same spirit as the TOPSIS-VIKOR divergence above, rather than as a defect to be corrected by changing methods until a target statistic is reached. Practically, this means the suitability pattern reported here for solar photovoltaic siting is primarily determined by infrastructure-accessibility criteria (slope, distance to grid, distance to water) rather than by GHI itself, a direct consequence of solar irradiance's low spatial variance across both study regions at the scale examined.

Five weighted criteria were used: solar irradiance or wind power density, technology-specific; slope; distance to existing transmission infrastructure; distance to water, derived from the OpenStreetMap waterways layer; and, implicitly through the exclusion mask described below, land use and protected-area status. Weights were taken from the GIS-MCDA renewable-siting literature rather than elicited from an internal expert panel, consistent with the goal of minimizing subjective weighting bias. The assigned weights were resource quality (solar irradiance or wind power density) 35 percent, proximity to infrastructure 25 percent, land availability 20 percent, and distance to grid 20 percent, summing to 100 percent. These values fall within the ranges documented across the reviewed GIS-MCDA siting literature (resource quality: 12 to 55 percent; grid proximity: 15 to 30 percent), with resource quality receiving the highest weight to reflect its primary role in determining hydrogen production cost, while the remaining weight was distributed across infrastructure and land access criteria. The robustness of the ranking to these weights was assessed by the plus-or-minus 20 percent weight-perturbation sensitivity described in section 2.7. Protected areas, water bodies, and built-up or urban land were treated as hard exclusion constraints via a binary mask, not as weighted criteria, since no defensible development could occur in these areas regardless of resource quality.

Four spatial thresholds bounded the suitability and site-aggregation procedure. A slope threshold of 15 degrees, intermediate between the more restrictive 10-degree limit applied in some solar-siting studies and the 20-degree limit used in wind-siting studies (Masurowski et al., 2017; Eberle et al., 2019), was applied uniformly to both technologies as a conservative shared constraint. The exclusion buffer applied around protected area boundaries is region-specific, reflecting a genuine difference in protected-area regime rather than a uniform precautionary margin. Northeast Brazil retains a 2-kilometre buffer, exceeding the minimum setback requirements documented in most Brazilian environmental licensing frameworks, consistent with a precautionary approach around the region's federal conservation units (unidades de conservação federais), which are strict no-use reserves. North Germany instead uses a 500-metre buffer: its protected-areas dataset consists of Natura 2000 sites, which under European Union law permit sustainable renewable-energy development subject to appropriate mitigation, so a uniform 2-kilometre buffer would exclude approximately 60 percent of the German study region, a result inconsistent with actual German renewable-energy siting practice, in which wind and solar deployment inside Natura 2000 boundaries with appropriate mitigation is documented by VDMA and Bundesnetzagentur (BNetzA) data. A maximum grid-connection distance of 50 kilometres excluded a site from the candidate pool beyond that distance, consistent with the practical upper limit for economically viable grid extension in technical-potential assessments (Masurowski et al., 2017); sites within 50 kilometres were scored continuously on the distance-to-grid criterion. A minimum contiguous suitable area of 50 square kilometres filtered out isolated cells and fragments unsuitable for a gigawatt-scale hydrogen hub footprint, while a maximum of 2,000 square kilometres prevented spatially extended uniform corridors from being retained as a single hub-scale site, with clusters exceeding this limit trimmed to their highest-suitability 2,000 square kilometre subset.

Sites were not selected ex ante. Because each region's boundary in this study is a single dissolved multi-state (Northeast Brazil) or multi-Land (Schleswig-Holstein and Niedersachsen) polygon rather than a set of municipality- or Landkreis-level administrative units, candidate sites were aggregated directly from the suitability raster as contiguous clusters of suitable cells rather than via administrative-unit zonal statistics. Within each region and technology's exclusion-masked suitability raster, only cells at or above the 85th percentile of the valid suitability distribution, cells passing the exclusion mask with a non-zero TOPSIS score, were retained as eligible for clustering, restricting the raster to its top 15 percent of cells before clustering began. Without this pre-filter, a comparatively uniform suitability surface over a large region merges under naive clustering into a small number of amorphous, sub-continental-scale patches with no meaningful correspondence to a gigawatt-scale hydrogen hub footprint. Contiguous groups of retained cells were then identified using 8-connectivity labelling, in which each cell is considered connected to its four orthogonal and four diagonal neighbours, and clusters whose total area fell below the 50 square kilometre minimum contiguous-area threshold were discarded. Any surviving cluster whose area still exceeded the 2,000 square kilometre maximum, for example an extended, spatially uniform coastal strip that remained contiguous even after the percentile pre-filter, was trimmed to its own highest-suitability 2,000 square kilometre subset of cells rather than discarded outright, a step that can split a single oversized cluster into several disconnected polygons. Each surviving cluster, or cluster fragment, was vectorized into its own polygon, with mean suitability, maximum suitability, and suitable area computed directly from its underlying raster cells. Clusters were ranked jointly by mean suitability score and suitable area, specifically by mean suitability multiplied by the natural logarithm of one plus the suitable area, a scoring rule that favours high suitability without letting a single small, isolated high-scoring patch outrank a large, moderately suitable cluster, and the top 20 ranked clusters per region and technology were retained as candidate hydrogen hub sites. The top 20 sites per region and technology were retained as candidate hydrogen hubs. A sensitivity check across shortlist sizes (10, 20, and 30 sites, run for the Northeast Brazil solar case) confirmed that the aggregate levelised cost of hydrogen result was insensitive to this threshold, with zero variance across the three shortlist sizes, validating the choice as sufficient for the study's comparative analysis. This exact invariance follows directly from the technical-potential model structure described in section 2.4: every candidate site within a given region and technology shares the same resolved capacity factor rather than a raster-derived, site-specific value, so each site's own levelised cost of hydrogen is algebraically identical regardless of which or how many sites are included, and the area-weighted mean across the shortlist is therefore unaffected by the shortlist size. The shortlist size does, correspondingly, scale the reported total installable capacity and annual hydrogen production roughly linearly, from 41.6 megatonnes per year at 10 sites to 112.9 megatonnes per year at 30 sites for the Northeast Brazil solar case tested; the 20-site threshold was retained on computational-tractability grounds for the downstream techno-economic assessment, not because a larger shortlist would alter the headline cost comparison.

2.4 Technical hydrogen potential

For each retained administrative unit, technical hydrogen production potential was estimated as suitable area, in square kilometres, multiplied by a technology-specific power density, in megawatts per square kilometre, drawn from a literature-validated range of 43 to 60 MW/km2 (baseline 51.5 MW/km2) for utility-scale solar photovoltaics and 4.1 to 13.7 MW/km2 (baseline 8.9 MW/km2) for onshore wind, values consistent with typical land-use intensity figures reported in the renewable siting literature. The solar range is derived from empirical studies of utility-scale fixed-tilt photovoltaic installations in the United States (Ong et al., 2013; Bolinger and Bolinger, 2022) and the meta-analysis of power densities across generation technologies by Van Zalk and Behrens (2018); the baseline of 51.5 MW/km2 represents the midpoint of the empirically observed range and is applied uniformly to both regions, since installable density follows from panel packing and inverter configuration rather than regional resource quality. The wind range is derived from Archer et al. (2019), Van Zalk and Behrens (2018), and standard turbine-spacing technical-potential models consistent with those used in NREL assessments, reflecting genuine variation in installed density across turbine generations, spacing conventions, and terrain types; the same range is applied to both regions since turbine-spacing physics are not region-specific. Both figures yielded installable capacity.

Annual energy yield was obtained by multiplying installable capacity by a technology- and region-specific capacity factor, likewise drawn from a literature-validated range (see section 3.1 below), rather than a single fixed figure. Baseline capacity factors were 24 percent (solar) and 36 percent (wind) for Northeast Brazil, and 12 percent (solar) and 32 percent (wind) for North Germany. The Brazil solar figure was derived from long-term Global Horizontal Irradiance averages for the Northeast Brazil region documented in the Global Solar Atlas (World Bank/ESMAP) and corroborated by Pereira and Lima (2008); it is deliberately conservative relative to best-site irradiance values, representing a resource-weighted average across the full suitability-filtered area rather than individually optimal sites. The Brazil wind figure was derived from long-term Global Wind Atlas averages at 100-metre hub height for the region's coastal and semi-arid interior zones, corroborated by IRENA (2024) and Brändle, Schönfisch and Schulte (2021); although IRENA (2025) documents newly commissioned Northeast Brazil projects achieving capacity factors of up to 56 percent, the baseline reflects a long-term atlas average across the full suitable area rather than frontier-project performance, consistent with this study's technical-potential methodology. The Germany solar figure is consistent with long-term irradiance averages for Schleswig-Holstein and Niedersachsen reported by Pfenninger and Staffell (2016) from 30 years of validated reanalysis data, corroborated by IRENA (2024). The Germany wind figure is consistent with the 28 to 35 percent range reported by IRENA (2024, 2025) for modern onshore installations in Northern Germany with hub heights above 100 metres, and with EWI (2020) long-term supply-cost modelling assumptions. Hydrogen output, in tonnes per year, was obtained by dividing annual energy yield by the electrolyzer specific energy consumption, converted to tonnes.

All hydrogen production in this study was assumed dedicated to electrolysis, with no competing grid off-take, no transmission-limit rule, and no curtailment-capture pathway. Capacity factors were drawn from long-term resource-atlas averages rather than from an hourly dispatch simulation, since the central claim of the study concerned a static resource-versus-finance decomposition rather than intra-year variability, grid curtailment, or storage sizing.

2.5 Techno-economic model: levelised cost of hydrogen

LCOH was computed with a standard discounted lifetime cost model:

LCOH equals the discounted sum of capital and operating expenditure over the project lifetime, divided by the discounted sum of hydrogen output over the same period, both discounted at the weighted average cost of capital.

Proton exchange membrane, PEM, electrolysis was used as the baseline technology, reflecting its superior load-following characteristics relative to alkaline systems. Alkaline electrolysis was retained as an explicit sensitivity scenario, with its own capital expenditure of 700 USD/kW, consistent with IRENA (2020)'s range for commercial alkaline systems above 10 MW and corroborated by IEA (2022), and specific energy consumption of 51 kWh/kg, reflecting mature commercial alkaline technology (IRENA, 2020) and conservatively set slightly above 2025 performance targets of approximately 49 kWh/kg. Alkaline stack replacement was set at 10 percent of capital expenditure at 90,000 operating hours; some sources (World Bank/ESMAP, 2026) report the alkaline stack as representing up to 45 percent of total system capital expenditure, and the 10 percent figure used here is interpreted as the periodic service-replacement cost, not the stack's full share of system cost. Baseline PEM capital expenditure was set at 1,200 USD/kW, within the 700 to 1,400 USD/kW range reported by IRENA (2020) and adjusted upward to reflect the post-2021 supply-chain cost inflation documented by the IEA (2023); the World Bank/ESMAP (2026) technical report on electrolyzer characteristics corroborates this as a defensible 2023 to 2025 baseline for commercial-scale PEM systems.

Specific energy consumption for PEM was set at 52 kWh/kg H2, the consensus baseline for commercial PEM systems in the 2023 to 2025 period, consistent with IRENA (2020) and with the 52.6 kWh/kg value reported by Glenk and Reichelstein (2019), equivalent to approximately 64 percent efficiency on a lower-heating-value basis. Fixed operating expenditure was set at 2 percent of capital expenditure per year, consistent with IRENA (2020) and corroborated by the absolute cost figure of approximately 23 to 24 USD/kW per year implied by German project-level data reviewed for this study, with stack replacement equal to 15 percent of capital expenditure at 80,000 operating hours, following IRENA (2020); some sources (World Bank/ESMAP, 2026) report the PEM stack as representing up to 25 percent of total system capital expenditure, and as with the alkaline figure above, the 15 percent replacement-cost figure is interpreted as the service cost of stack replacement (labour, ancillaries, and stack hardware net of reuse credits) rather than the stack's full share of system cost. A country-specific capital expenditure multiplier of 1.15 was applied to Brazil's electrolyzer capital expenditure, reflecting import duties on electrolyzer equipment, freight differentials relative to the European domestic supply chain, and local content requirements documented in Brazilian energy sector procurement, and 1.00 to Germany's; the 1.15 value is consistent with the cost-of-system differentials implied by IRENA (2024)'s regional levelised-cost-of-electricity spread between Brazil and comparable European markets. This multiplier is applied to electrolyzer capital expenditure only and is not applied to renewable generation capital expenditure, which carries no country-specific multiplier in this model. Water cost was set at 0.05 USD/kg H2 in both regions, pending local verification.

Renewable generation capital and operating expenditure entered the model as inputs to the levelised cost of electricity feeding the electrolyzer, resolved by technology and region: Germany's solar and onshore wind capital expenditure now follow Abuzayed and Hartmann (2022), while Brazil retains this study's earlier global-benchmark figures via an explicit region-specific override, so the two countries' renewable capital costs no longer share one nominal global baseline. Solar photovoltaic capital expenditure for Germany was set at 660 USD/kW (range 500 to 900 USD/kW), converted from Abuzayed and Hartmann (2022)'s reported 600 EUR/kW at a fixed EUR/USD rate of 1.10. Onshore wind capital expenditure for Germany was set at 1,317 USD/kW (range 1,000 to 1,700 USD/kW), converted from the same source's reported 1,197 EUR/kW at the same fixed rate. Brazil's solar photovoltaic capital expenditure remains 700 USD/kW (range 500 to 1,000 USD/kW), consistent with the global weighted-average utility-scale solar cost of 758 USD/kW reported by IRENA (2024) for 2023, and Brazil's onshore wind capital expenditure remains 1,050 USD/kW (range 800 to 1,550 USD/kW), reflecting IRENA (2024)'s reported Brazilian onshore wind cost of approximately 1,099 USD/kW for 2023, one of the lowest markets globally, and consistent with EPE auction-clearing cost benchmarks for Northeast Brazil projects; both are held unchanged deliberately, preserved as an explicit override rather than left to silently track Germany's updated figures. Fixed operating expenditure was set at 1.5 percent of capital expenditure per year for solar and 2.0 percent for onshore wind, within the 1.0 to 1.5 percent and 2.0 to 3.0 percent ranges documented by IRENA (2023, 2024) and EWI (2020), with a project lifetime of 25 years for both technologies, the standard assumption in IRENA long-term cost modelling.

Germany's solar and onshore wind capacity factor ranges were likewise updated to Abuzayed and Hartmann (2022): 9.2 to 14.6 percent for solar (baseline unchanged at 12.0 percent) and 19.6 to 36.5 percent for onshore wind (baseline unchanged at 32.0 percent). Brazil's onshore wind capacity factor range was separately updated to 30 to 42 percent (baseline unchanged at 36.0 percent), reflecting IRENA (2024)'s finding that Brazil achieved the highest global weighted-average capacity factor for newly commissioned onshore wind, 56 percent, driven by modern 4 to 6 MW turbines at 120 to 150 metre hub heights, together with MDPI (2025) operational data for the ten largest Brazilian wind farms (Chafariz, Paraíba, 43 percent; Assuruá, Bahia, 41 percent; Lagoa dos Ventos, Piauí, 39 percent; Alto do Sertão, Bahia, 41 percent; Rio do Vento, Rio Grande do Norte, 37 percent; Chuí, Rio Grande do Sul, older turbines, 35 percent). The 56 percent global-high figure was deliberately excluded from the baseline as a newest-fleet outlier not yet generalizable across the region's installed base; the revised baseline of 36 percent is a conservative operational-farm average that also reflects this study's candidate sites sitting closer to 100 metre hub heights than the 120 to 150 metre range behind IRENA's headline figure. The previous 30 to 36 percent range was inconsistent with both this operational evidence and IRENA's global benchmark. Both regions' updated ranges, and every source above, are cross-referenced in `docs/memory/05_economic_model.md` and `docs/memory/09_methodology_assumptions.md`.

Rather than a single blended country-level WACC, this study assigned WACC separately by generation technology within each country, reflecting the systematic spread documented in the literature between solar and wind financing costs within the same jurisdiction, for example Germany's WACC for utility-scale solar photovoltaics was reported at approximately 2.7 percent, compared with 3.0 to 6.1 percent for onshore and offshore wind (Steffen, 2020). Baseline WACC values used were 11.0 percent, solar, and 12.0 percent, wind, for Northeast Brazil, and 3.0 percent, solar, and 4.0 percent, wind, for North Germany, each with an associated sensitivity range drawn from the reviewed literature ([8.0, 16.0] percent for Brazil; [2.0, 7.0] percent for Germany). Brazil's baseline reflects financing conditions documented for renewable energy projects in emerging markets with Brazil's sovereign risk profile, consistent with the 10 to 13 percent nominal WACC range reported for Brazilian renewable assets in IRENA (2023, 2024), Egli, Steffen and Schmidt (2018), and Steffen (2020), and with the country-risk premium decomposition applied to hydrogen export cost modelling by Terrapon-Pfaff et al. (2025) for comparable emerging markets; the one-percentage-point solar-below-wind spread mirrors the ordering documented across mature and emerging markets by Steffen (2020) and confirmed for the Brazilian context by IRENA (2024). Germany's baseline is consistent with the 2.7 to 3.1 percent solar and 3.0 to 4.0 percent wind ranges reported by Steffen (2020) for German utility-scale assets under pre-2022 stable macroeconomic conditions, broadly corroborated by IRENA (2023, 2024) for Western European markets. The resulting spread of approximately 8 percentage points between the two countries at baseline, consistent with the emerging-market financing premium documented in Egli, Steffen and Schmidt (2018) and Brändle, Schönfisch and Schulte (2021), constitutes the central tension this study's decomposition and inversion-point analysis (section 2.6) is designed to quantify.

Consistent with the absence of an operational, universal per-kilogram production credit in either country as of 2026, the baseline scenario applied no policy incentive in either region. Two hypothetical incentive scenarios were run as explicit sensitivity cases: a Brazilian production credit under the REHIDRO framework, and a production credit in the 2.00 to 4.50 EUR/kg range for Germany over a ten-year horizon, spanning conservative project-specific IPCEI Hy2Use support levels to the ceiling price observed in the European Hydrogen Bank's first auction round; full detail, including the resolved baseline value within this range, is given in section 2.8. The LCOH computed throughout covered production only, at the point of electrolysis; transport, compression, liquefaction, storage, and downstream conversion, for example to ammonia, were excluded from the cost boundary.

2.6 Counterfactual decomposition, combined scenarios, and the competitiveness frontier

Three model runs were executed for each retained site, holding all parameters other than the manipulated variable fixed at baseline values. The actual-conditions run applied each region's own resource profile, its own technology-specific WACC, and its own capital expenditure multiplier, producing the baseline LCOH comparison between regions. The WACC-swap counterfactual retained each region's own resource profile and capital expenditure multiplier but exchanged WACC values between regions, Brazil evaluated at Germany's WACC and Germany evaluated at Brazil's WACC, isolating the financial-cost effect while holding the resource effect and the capital-cost-multiplier effect constant.

Combined scenarios. Every uncertainty range in the model, weighted average cost of capital, capacity factor, capacity density, and renewable generation capital expenditure, is stored as a literature-validated minimum, baseline, and maximum. Earlier drafts of this pipeline resolved these ranges independently, varying only capacity factor across a min/baseline/max sweep while holding WACC and capital expenditure fixed at baseline. The pipeline now instead defines and always executes three combined scenarios, in which every one of these parameters moves simultaneously in the same direction: the minimum (worst-case) combined scenario pulls WACC to its literature-reported maximum, capacity factor and capacity density to their minimum, and renewable capital expenditure to its maximum, all at once; the maximum (best-case) combined scenario pulls every one of those same parameters to its opposite, most favourable extreme; the baseline scenario resolves every parameter at its own baseline value. This is a deliberate departure from an independent, one-at-a-time treatment of each parameter's uncertainty band, which the one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis of section 2.7 already provides: stacking every parameter's worst case simultaneously produces a coherent, physically interpretable worst-case envelope for the levelised cost of hydrogen, rather than an artificially narrow range that would result from letting favourable and unfavourable draws of different parameters cancel out. The pipeline always executes all three combined scenarios in sequence for every region and technology pair and reports levelised cost of hydrogen as a range bounded by the minimum and maximum combined scenarios, with the baseline scenario reported as the central estimate.

2D WACC competitiveness frontier. The one-dimensional inversion-point search used in earlier drafts of this study, which varied only Brazil's weighted average cost of capital against Germany's fixed baseline, has been replaced by a two-dimensional competitiveness frontier spanning both countries' financing-cost uncertainty simultaneously. For each renewable technology, a deterministic grid of fifteen to twenty evenly spaced points is constructed along each country's own literature-reported weighted-average-cost-of-capital range, and the levelised cost of hydrogen is computed at every combination of Brazilian and German weighted average cost of capital in that grid, with every other parameter, capacity factor, capacity density, renewable capital expenditure, operating expenditure, project lifetime, and electrolyzer specification, held at its own baseline value. A grid point is flagged as a parity point when the absolute difference between the two countries' levelised cost of hydrogen falls within five percent of their mean, a relative tolerance chosen to be wide enough to trace a continuous frontier across the discrete grid while remaining a materially tight cost-parity band. Two policy-relevant subsidy-equivalence metrics are reported for each technology from this frontier: a WACC-gap closure value, the Brazilian weighted average cost of capital, holding Germany's own weighted average cost of capital fixed at its baseline, at which the two countries' levelised cost of hydrogen are equal, together with the resulting change in Brazil's levelised cost of hydrogen relative to its own baseline; and a direct support value, the flat per-kilogram production credit that would close the baseline cost gap between the two countries without any change in financing cost at all. The first metric is interpreted as the value of a definancing-cost reform (de-risking instruments, concessional financing, sovereign guarantees) sufficient to reach parity; the second as the value of an equivalent direct production subsidy achieving the same outcome without any financing-cost reform.

For solar photovoltaics, Brazil's baseline levelised cost of hydrogen, 7.34 USD/kg, is already below Germany's baseline, 7.74 USD/kg, at the current baseline weighted average cost of capital of 11.0 percent for Brazil and 3.0 percent for Germany: Brazil is competitive today, not merely at a future financing-cost threshold. Root-finding along the slice of the frontier grid at Germany's baseline weighted average cost of capital confirms that Brazil's solar weighted average cost of capital could rise to 11.92 percent, a Brazilian financing-cost headroom of 0.92 percentage points above its current baseline, before its levelised cost of hydrogen would rise to match Germany's baseline; equivalently, the direct-support value at baseline is negative, approximately negative 0.40 USD/kg, confirming that no production subsidy is required for Brazil to remain competitive with Germany in solar hydrogen production under current baseline financing conditions. Both figures are numerically identical to the Brazilian weighted-average-cost-of-capital threshold and the levelised-cost-of-hydrogen values reported for solar under the earlier one-dimensional inversion-point search, confirming the two-dimensional frontier reduces to the same underlying result along that one slice.

For onshore wind, no parity point exists anywhere on the tested frontier grid: Brazil's wind levelised cost of hydrogen, 6.10 USD/kg at baseline, remains above Germany's baseline wind levelised cost of hydrogen, 4.16 USD/kg, across the entire tested combination of Brazilian and German weighted average cost of capital, so Brazil is reported as always more expensive than Germany for onshore wind, a valid frontier result rather than a search failure. The direct-support value required to close this gap at baseline financing conditions is approximately 1.95 USD/kg, materially larger than the solar figure, reflecting that Brazil's own baseline wind weighted average cost of capital and capital expenditure both sit above their solar counterparts in absolute terms, structurally offsetting Brazil's genuinely superior wind resource quality (a 36 percent baseline capacity factor against Germany's 32 percent) across the entire financing-cost band examined. This asymmetry between the solar and wind results, one technology already competitive at baseline financing conditions, the other requiring a substantial direct subsidy to reach parity even under the most favourable financing-cost combination tested, is reported as a substantive finding of the decomposition, not a methodological artefact.

The competitiveness-frontier results reported above are conditional on the renewable capital-expenditure and capacity-factor assumptions described in section 2.5, held at their own baseline values throughout the frontier grid; the combined min/baseline/max scenario ranges introduced above instead vary those parameters together with the weighted average cost of capital, reported separately as a levelised-cost-of-hydrogen range per region and technology (section 3.1). Section 2.7 separately reports a global Sobol sensitivity analysis quantifying how much of the variance in levelised cost of hydrogen is attributable to weighted average cost of capital relative to capacity factor and renewable capital expenditure.

2.7 Sensitivity analysis

A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis was conducted on the parameters with the largest documented uncertainty ranges: WACC, by country and technology; PEM capital expenditure; electrolyzer specific energy consumption; and the country-specific capital expenditure multiplier. Electrolyzer technology, PEM versus alkaline, was included as an additional sensitivity dimension, using the parameter values given in section 2.5. Independently, a weight-perturbation check was applied to the TOPSIS criterion weights, each weight varied plus or minus 20 percent in isolation, holding the others fixed, and the resulting change in the ranking of top-suitability administrative units was evaluated against the VIKOR cross-check described in section 2.3, confirming that the suitability result was robust both to the choice of MCDA method and to the specific weights used within TOPSIS.

A global variance-based sensitivity analysis using Sobol indices (Saltelli et al., 2008, 2019) complemented the one-at-a-time sweep, conducted on the three parameters with the largest documented uncertainty ranges in the LCOH model: WACC (region- and technology-specific range), capacity factor (region- and technology-specific range), and renewable generation capital expenditure (technology-specific range). Each parameter range was drawn directly from the literature-validated bounds stored in the model configuration. A total of 5,120 model evaluations were used per region and technology combination, generated via Saltelli sampling with 1,024 base samples for three parameters, providing sufficient convergence for first-order and total-order indices in a near-additive model, confirmed by the sum of first-order indices approximating 0.99 across all four region and technology pairs. Electrolyzer capital and operating expenditure were excluded from the Sobol analysis because no literature-validated range exists for these parameters in the model configuration, where they are stored as single scalars; capacity density was excluded because the LCOH formula itself has no capacity or area parameter, since density enters only the technical-potential calculation described in section 2.4. WACC had the highest first-order index in three of the four region and technology pairs (Brazil solar and wind, Germany wind), while capacity factor was highest for Germany solar, reflecting that region and technology's comparatively narrow WACC range relative to its capacity-factor range.

2.8 Incentive scenario analysis

Earlier drafts of this study modeled Brazil's REHIDRO programme and Germany's IPCEI Hy2Use support as if each were a flat per-kilogram production credit — 1.00 USD/kg over ten years for Brazil, 2.00 to 4.50 EUR/kg over ten years for Germany (midpoint 3.25 EUR/kg, converted at a 2024 calendar-year average EUR/USD reference rate of 1.0822) — used as illustrative placeholders in the absence of a confirmed per-kilogram figure. Both values have since been zeroed in the computational pipeline, and, on closer examination of the actual programme designs, neither region's real incentive landscape resembles a flat per-kilogram production credit at all: REHIDRO is a capital-expenditure-side tax suspension, not a production subsidy, and Germany's own instruments (IPCEI, H2Global, carbon contracts for difference) are investment and offtake mechanisms, not per-kilogram credits. Continuing to model either as a fabricated flat credit — even a clearly labeled placeholder — risked being read as evidence that such a credit exists or is imminent. `region_cfg.incentives.production_credit_usd_per_kg` is therefore 0.0 for both regions, and the REHIDRO/IPCEI Hy2Use scenario functions in `src/economics/incentive_scenarios.py` now report LCOH under that zero credit — bit-for-bit identical to the zero-incentive baseline in section 2.6 — rather than a fabricated non-zero reduction. This does not affect the baseline LCOH comparison in section 2.6, which was already computed with no incentive applied by construction.

In place of the retired placeholder credits, this section instead reports what a hypothetical flat per-kilogram credit WOULD need to be to close the baseline LCOH gap identified in section 2.6 — an "incentive equivalence" figure, not a claim about an existing policy instrument — computed directly from the already-reported LCOH gap (`src/economics/competitiveness_frontier.py`'s `direct_support_range()`) rather than re-derived or invented separately. For onshore wind, Brazil's baseline LCOH exceeds Germany's by 1.92 USD/kg, so a 1.92 USD/kg credit would be required to reach parity today — a figure REHIDRO and the future PHBC programme (see section 2.8.1) do not currently provide in this form. For solar photovoltaics, Brazil's baseline LCOH is already below Germany's by 0.26 USD/kg, so no credit is needed for Brazil to remain competitive; the equivalence figure in this case is reported as a natural cost advantage, not a gap to close. Section 3.1 additionally reports this same gap computed at each of the three combined min/baseline/max scenarios (section 2.6), since the sign and magnitude of the gap are not fixed across scenarios — for solar, the gap ranges from a 0.90 USD/kg Brazilian advantage at the best-case scenario to a 2.92 USD/kg gap requiring support at the worst-case scenario, illustrating that solar's baseline competitiveness is not robust to the full literature-reported uncertainty range on both sides.

2.8.1 Incentive Landscape and Policy Implications

Germany's hydrogen policy support operates through project-specific and market-level instruments rather than a production credit. The IPCEI Hy2Use programme (Important Project of Common European Interest) has approved 62 projects across participating EU member states, providing state-aid-cleared investment aid toward capital expenditure on electrolysis, hydrogen application, and infrastructure projects — support tied to individual project investment plans, not a running per-kilogram output payment. H2Global operates as an import market-maker: a EUR900 million (German federal) funding instrument that purchases hydrogen and hydrogen derivatives on the world market at the price required to secure supply and resells them to European offtakers at the (lower) price the market will bear, absorbing the difference — a double-auction mechanism that de-risks offtake for producers and importers, not a domestic per-kilogram production subsidy. Carbon Contracts for Difference (CCfD), a third instrument under discussion and partial implementation in Germany's industrial decarbonisation policy, guarantee a fixed effective carbon price to industrial users switching to low-carbon processes (including hydrogen-based ones), paying the difference between that guaranteed price and the prevailing EU ETS carbon price — a carbon-price hedge, not a hydrogen production credit either. None of the three publishes, or is structured to produce, a single published per-kilogram-of-hydrogen cost-reduction figure; this study does not compute or invent one, for the same reason the direct-support figures in this section are reported as a hypothetical requirement rather than a description of existing support.

Brazil's hydrogen policy support is at an earlier stage and structured differently again. REHIDRO, part of the Brazilian federal government's broader green hydrogen policy framework, provides a five-year suspension of PIS/Pasep and Cofins federal taxes on capital goods and services purchased for green hydrogen production projects (2025 to 2030) — a capital-expenditure-side tax relief mechanism, not a per-kilogram production subsidy, and structurally closer to Germany's IPCEI investment aid than to a production credit. The Programa Brasil Hidrogênio Verde (Programa Hidrogênio de Baixo Carbono, PHBC) is a further, larger federal programme — R$18.3 billion in support allocated for 2028 to 2032 — but is not yet operational as of this writing and, even once active, is not confirmed to take the form of a flat per-kilogram production credit; details of its eventual instrument design were not available at the time of this study. Neither REHIDRO nor the PHBC currently provides, or is confirmed to provide in the future, the specific form of support this study's direct-support metric describes.

The direct support figures reported in this study — including the scenario-range figures in section 3.1 — represent the hypothetical per-kilogram production credit that would be required to achieve LCOH parity between Brazil and Germany, not an existing policy instrument in either country. They are a policy-relevant benchmark for evaluating a potential future production-credit design (in either country), not a description, prediction, or endorsement of REHIDRO, the PHBC, IPCEI Hy2Use, H2Global, or CCfD as currently structured.

2.9 Limitations

The spatial suitability analysis functioned as a supporting characterization of where hydrogen hubs could plausibly be located; the central empirical claim of the study was the LCOH decomposition and competitiveness-frontier result, not the suitability ranking itself. Land-use exclusion was implemented as a binary mask rather than a fully harmonized multi-class comparison between the MapBiomas and ESA WorldCover taxonomies. Capacity factors were drawn from long-term resource-atlas averages rather than from an hourly dispatch simulation; the study therefore did not characterize intra-year variability, storage requirements, or grid-curtailment dynamics, a deliberate scope boundary rather than an oversight.

The land-use exclusion layer for Germany was derived from ESA WorldCover 2021 rather than the Corine Land Cover product originally planned. The Copernicus Land Monitoring Service token-authenticated endpoint was unavailable during data acquisition, and WorldCover was adopted as a harmonized alternative with global coverage and comparable spatial resolution. This substitution may affect direct comparability with studies using Corine-specific class definitions, particularly for mixed agricultural and semi-natural land categories. The exclusion mask applied, urban, water bodies, and wetlands, is consistent across both the MapBiomas, Brazil, and WorldCover, Germany, taxonomies and does not materially alter the suitability results, but fine-grained class-level interpretation should account for the source difference.

Administrative boundaries used for Brazil, IBGE, and for Germany, GADM, were drawn from sources with different curation and update processes. The choice of GADM for Germany prioritized full acquisition automation over the granularity and currency offered by national cartographic sources such as BKG VG250. This may have introduced imprecision in the delineation of fine-grained administrative units used in the zonal aggregation described in section 2.3, and should be considered when interpreting cross-country comparisons of administrative-unit rankings.

WACC, even where differentiated by technology, remained a simplification of a genuinely heterogeneous and partly confidential financing landscape (Steffen, 2020), and the evidentiary basis for the Brazilian solar and wind technology split was weaker in the literature reviewed than for the German split. The competitiveness-frontier grid is deterministic and finite (fifteen to twenty steps per axis), so the reported parity points and subsidy-equivalence values are subject to the same grid resolution as any other discretized frontier; the WACC-gap closure metric's root-finding step recovers a continuous value along the tested slice, but the parity region reported directly from the grid is necessarily an approximation to the true continuous frontier. Finally, the analysis was restricted to production-stage LCOH; its conclusions should not be extended to delivered, transported or converted, hydrogen cost without further work.

3.1 Parameter ranges and uncertainty propagation

Weighted average cost of capital, capacity factor, capacity density, and renewable generation capital expenditure are each stored as a literature-validated range with three named points, a minimum, a baseline, and a maximum, per region and technology (weighted average cost of capital and capacity factor) or per technology (capacity density and renewable capital expenditure) rather than as a single fixed figure. Rather than a single top-level scenario selector resolving one branch for an entire run, as in earlier drafts of this pipeline, the computational pipeline always executes three combined scenarios in sequence, described in section 2.6: a minimum (worst-case) scenario in which every one of these parameters is pulled to its least favourable extreme simultaneously, a maximum (best-case) scenario in which every parameter is pulled to its most favourable extreme simultaneously, and the baseline scenario. This combined treatment does not vary one parameter in isolation, as the one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis of section 2.7 already does; it regenerates every downstream result, technical potential, LCOH, and decomposition alike, at each scenario's coherent parameter combination, so that the study's central comparison can be reported as a range bounded by a defensible worst-case and best-case envelope rather than as a single point estimate or as the narrower, potentially misleading range that would result from varying only one parameter while holding the others at baseline.

Capacity factor ranges are region- and technology-specific, reflecting the same resource-quality contrast between Northeast Brazil and North Germany that motivates the study's overall research design (section 2.1): baseline values of 24 percent, solar, and 36 percent, wind, for Northeast Brazil, against 12 percent, solar, and 32 percent, wind, for North Germany, each with an associated literature-reported minimum and maximum. Capacity density ranges are technology-specific only, 43 to 60 MW/km2 for solar photovoltaics and 4.1 to 13.7 MW/km2 for onshore wind, since installable density follows from land-use intensity and turbine engineering rather than from regional resource quality. Full rationale, literature sources, and the complete set of study-specific values are documented in `docs/memory/09_methodology_assumptions.md` (capacity factor) and `docs/memory/10_capacity_density_assumptions.md` (capacity density).

The direct-support metric introduced in section 2.8 (the hypothetical flat per-kilogram credit that would close the baseline LCOH gap) is likewise computed at each of the three combined scenarios, not baseline alone, since both countries' WACC, capacity factor, and renewable CAPEX move together at each scenario and the resulting gap is not guaranteed to be largest or smallest at any particular scenario a priori. For onshore wind, Brazil requires a production incentive at every tested scenario, ranging from 1.41 USD/kg (best case) to 5.59 USD/kg (worst case), bracketing the baseline figure of 1.92 USD/kg. For solar photovoltaics, the range straddles zero: from a 0.90 USD/kg Brazilian cost advantage at the best-case scenario to a 2.92 USD/kg gap requiring support at the worst-case scenario, bracketing the baseline advantage of 0.26 USD/kg — demonstrating that solar's baseline competitiveness, while real today, is not robust across the full literature-reported uncertainty range on both countries' financing costs, capacity factors, and renewable capital expenditure simultaneously. Neither the sign nor the magnitude of either range was fixed in advance; both are reported as computed.
